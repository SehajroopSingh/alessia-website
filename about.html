About

I am interested in what happens when systems change faster than the people and institutions inside them.

As a consultant, I work at the intersection of ESG regulation, AI governance and socioeconomic development — moving between structural questions about how systems and institutions are designed and the individual question of how people actually experience and navigate them. In practice, that means working in three modes: analysing systems, translating them for the people who have to act on them, and sometimes building new ones.

That movement between the structural and the human runs through the projects that define my work so far: researching public-interest interventions with Dreamocracy; leading cross-sector research on AI governance and AI adoption; authoring a CSRD/ESRS-aligned sustainability disclosure report and the Tagetik data architecture beneath it; leading the stakeholder communication that makes regulation legible to the people it affects; and co-founding MemSurf, an AI-native learning application.

These are different fields on paper. In practice they are the same problem seen from different angles: a disclosure framework, a regulation, an AI system and a learning app are all systems that succeed or fail depending on how well they are designed around the people inside them.

I work on ESG and sustainability, AI, socioeconomic development and human behaviour — by analysing how the systems around them actually work, translating what I find for the people who have to act on it, and occasionally building new systems from scratch.

The deeper lens

Cognitive bias, and the veil we work through

Running through all of this is a deeper interest in cognitive bias as the lens through which we perceive and interpret the world. We do not observe systems from outside them; we perceive them through assumptions, incentives, experience and cognitive shortcuts.

Some philosophical traditions call this distance between reality and perception Maya — the veil through which we experience the world.

I treat this as an intellectual lens rather than a doctrine. Taking the veil seriously changes how you read a regulation, structure a report, or design a product: you stop asking only "is this system correct?" and start asking "how will a person, with limited attention and their own incentives, actually experience this?"

That conviction ultimately led me to co-found MemSurf. Studying how people learn, decide and misjudge is one thing; building a product whose success depends entirely on getting those things right is another. Entrepreneurship, for me, is not a departure from policy — it is first-hand exposure to the systems I study, and the most honest test of whether the ideas hold up.

Method

How I work

I move between disciplines.

Complex problems rarely respect disciplinary boundaries.

I start with the system.

Understand the structure, incentives and institutional context.

Then I look at the person.

Understand perception, behaviour, incentives and lived experience.

I translate complexity.

Research only becomes useful when it can inform decisions.

I want evidence.

Data, research and stakeholder perspectives should challenge assumptions.

I build when I can.

Sometimes the best way to understand a system is to enter it and make something.

Questions, answered

FAQ

What do you actually work on?

Consulting projects across ESG and sustainability reporting, AI governance research, socioeconomic development and behavioural communication — in three modes: analysing systems, translating them for the people who act on them, and sometimes building new ones. Concretely: a CSRD/ESRS-aligned sustainability report and its data architecture, six months of cross-sector AI governance research, public-interest policy research and a 70-person policy workshop — and, alongside all of it, building MemSurf.

How does AI fit into your work?

In two ways. As a researcher, I have studied how AI is governed and adopted — the EU AI Act, copyright and IP, misinformation, deepfakes and platform responsibility, through interviews with experts across technology and the audiovisual industries. As a builder, I co-founded an AI-native learning app. The two feed each other: governance work is sharper when you understand the technology from the inside.

Why sustainability?

Because sustainability is where regulation, data and organisational behaviour collide. Frameworks like the CSRD ask organisations to translate complex requirements into working internal systems — data collection, verification, double materiality, audit-ready documentation. That is a systems-design question, and it is exactly the kind of problem I find interesting.

Why behavioural science?

Because every system I work on is ultimately navigated by people. Policies are interpreted, reports are read, technologies are adopted — or not — based on perception, incentives and cognitive shortcuts. Behavioural science is not a side interest; it is the lens through which I approach institutions and technology.

What does building MemSurf have to do with policy?

It is the same question approached from inside: designing a system around how people actually think, learn and decide. Building it gives me first-hand exposure to product decisions, user behaviour, analytics and experimentation — the realities of emerging technology that policy work usually observes from the outside. I think that makes me a more useful policy professional, not a different one.

Are you a policy analyst, ESG specialist or AI governance professional?

By training, a policy analyst. In practice, the work spans all three. The common denominator is the same in each case: how institutions and people respond when the systems around them change.

What connects all these different fields?

One question: how do we design systems that work better for the people who have to navigate them? Policy, ESG reporting, AI governance, behavioural science and product-building are different entry points into that question.

What kind of organisations do you want to work with?

European and international institutions, think tanks, policy consultancies, research institutions, and sustainability, ESG and responsible-technology teams — anyone working seriously at the intersection of regulation, technology and human behaviour.
